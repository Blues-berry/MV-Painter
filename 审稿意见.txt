Summary This paper investigates the effect of geometry-adapter scaling in multi-view diffusion-based 3D texture generation. The authors observe a shape-texture trade-off: increasing the adapter scale generally improves structural alignment metrics, while overly strong geometric conditioning may suppress local color variation and texture details. Based on this observation, the paper proposes Timestep-Conditioned Adapter Scaling (TCAS), a training-free inference strategy that applies relatively weak adapter scaling during the early and late denoising stages and stronger scaling during the middle stage. The final C3 configuration, (1.25,2.50,1.25), is selected from 17 variants on a 24-object probe set and then evaluated on a 300-object validation set. The paper further supports its observations using texture-related diagnostics, CLIP-IQA, visual comparisons, and a blinded preference study. Strengths 1. The paper identifies a practically relevant failure mode of geometry-conditioned texture generation. In particular, the authors show that improvements in structural metrics such as FG-SSIM do not necessarily correspond to improved visual quality, and aggressive adapter scaling can lead to texture flattening and reduced local appearance variation. The manual audit reports this behavior for 24 out of 26 examined objects. 2. The experimental protocol is carefully controlled and relatively comprehensive. The paper fixes the base UNet, VAE, adapter weights, random seeds, views, and sampling budget when comparing scaling strategies, and separates schedule selection on a 24-object probe set from validation on 300 objects. 3. The evaluation does not rely on a single structural or texture metric. The authors complement structural metrics with multiple texture diagnostics, CLIP-IQA, qualitative comparisons, and a blinded preference study, which makes the empirical evidence for the reported trade-off more convincing. Weaknesses 1. The generalizability of the core observation and TCAS is insufficiently demonstrated. All major experiments are conducted on a single MVPainter-style geometry-conditioned multi-view diffusion pipeline. Therefore, the current results cannot distinguish whether the reported early/middle/late scaling behavior is a general property of geometry-conditioned diffusion adapters or merely a characteristic of this particular backbone, adapter architecture, and sampling configuration. This is especially important because the paper makes broader claims about adapter-specific temporal conflicts and stage-dependent adapter control. 2. The technical novelty of TCAS is limited, and its design remains largely heuristic. The main method reduces to a manually specified three-stage low-high-low schedule, with fixed one-third stage boundaries and the selected values (1.25,2.50,1.25). The paper does not provide sufficient analysis explaining why these stage boundaries should be meaningful or transferable across models, samplers, object categories, or materials. While comparisons with several generic scheduling strategies are useful, they mainly demonstrate that C3 works better for the current pipeline rather than establishing a generally applicable inference principle. 3. Some experimental and reproducibility details remain insufficiently specified. The manuscript does not clearly describe the source and construction of the different object subsets or their possible overlap. In addition, the FAC extension introduces learned correction modules, but the manuscript provides limited information about its training data, optimization setup, and train/validation separation. These details are important for interpreting the reported FAC improvements and reproducing the experiments. Justification Overall, I find the empirical observation practically relevant and the experiments reasonably comprehensive. However, my main concern is that all major conclusions are established on a single MVPainter-style geometry-conditioned diffusion pipeline. Given the simplicity of the proposed three-stage scaling rule, it remains unclear whether TCAS captures a general property of geometry-conditioned diffusion models or is mainly a model-specific hyperparameter schedule. This substantially limits the generality and significance of the current contribution.

Questions

This manuscript investigates an important and often overlooked issue in geometry-conditioned multi-view diffusion: increasing adapter strength can improve structural alignment while reducing local texture variation. The proposed Timestep-Conditioned Adapter Scaling (TCAS) strategy is simple, training-free, and practically useful. The controlled adapter-scale analysis, the separation between the 24-object probe set and the 300-object validation set, and the combined use of structural metrics, texture diagnostics, CLIP-IQA, qualitative comparisons, and human preference evaluation are strengths of the work. The manuscript is also generally clear and well organized. I have the following comments that would help improve the clarity and completeness of the paper. 1.Competing timestep scheduling strategies are not validated on the large-scale independent set. Representative scheduling strategies, including linear decay, cosine decay, linear warm-up, and cosine bump, are compared only on the 24-object probe set, whereas the 300-object validation experiment includes only two uniform-scale baselines ( s = 1.25 and s = 2.50 ) together with TCAS. Therefore, the large-scale experiment supports the advantage of TCAS over fixed scaling, but does not yet establish that its advantage over alternative timestep schedules generalizes to the independent validation set. I suggest evaluating at least representative competitors such as cosine bump and linear warm-up on the 300-object validation set. 2. The aggregation procedures used in Tables 6 and 7 appear to differ, but this is not clearly explained in the manuscript. For example, directly using the reported RGB Std values in Table 6 gives 0.0251/0.0408≈0.615 and 0.0377/0.0408≈0.924, whereas the corresponding retention ratios in Table 7 are 0.643 and 0.970. This does not necessarily indicate a calculation error, as the two tables may use different aggregation orders (e.g., ratio of means versus mean of per-object ratios). Please clarify the exact computation and aggregation procedure used for the retention ratios in Table 7 to improve the interpretability and reproducibility of the results. 3. The statistical reporting in the perceptual evaluation should be clarified. In Table 8, it is unclear whether the values following “±” represent standard deviation, standard error, or confidence intervals. In addition, the manuscript refers to a “paired test” and bootstrap confidence intervals without specifying the exact statistical test or bootstrap procedure. Please clarify the paired test used, the number of bootstrap resamples, and the confidence-interval construction method to improve the interpretability and reproducibility of the reported results. 4. More complete dataset and implementation details would improve the reproducibility of the work. In particular, the authors should clarify the source, category composition, object selection procedure, and possible overlap of the 50-object, 26-object, 24-object, and 300-object sets. In addition, it would be helpful to provide key implementation details, such as the model checkpoint, sampler, noise scheduler, image resolution, preprocessing pipeline, and the exact implementation of the evaluation metrics. For FAC, the manuscript should further specify the training data, loss function, optimization settings, additional parameter count, and whether the 300 validation objects are strictly excluded from the FAC training and model-selection process.
